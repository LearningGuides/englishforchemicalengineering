# 1. Transitions Between Sections

## Learning Objective

Use transitions to connect different sections of a technical presentation smoothly.

## What Is a Transition?

A **transition** tells the audience that one part of the presentation is ending and another is beginning.

An effective transition often follows this structure:

**Previous idea → Transition → New idea**

### Example: Shell-and-Tube Heat Exchanger

**Components → Operating principle**

> Now that we have identified the main components of the shell-and-tube heat exchanger, let's look at how it operates.

**Operating principle → Applications**

> Now that we have seen how the equipment operates, let's turn our attention to its industrial applications.

**Applications → Advantages and limitations**

> So far, we have examined some common applications. I'd now like to focus on the advantages and limitations of this equipment.

Useful transition expressions include:

- **Now that we have seen..., let's look at...**
- **Let's turn our attention to...**
- **I'd now like to focus on...**
- **So far, we have examined...**
- **The next point I'd like to discuss is...**

### Practice

Complete the transition:

> Now that we have examined the main components of the ____________, let's look at ______________________________.

# 2. Road Signs

## Learning Objective

Use linking expressions to show the relationship between technical ideas.

## What Are Road Signs?

**Road signs** are linking expressions that guide listeners through a presentation. They indicate whether the speaker is adding information, changing direction, contrasting ideas, or explaining a result.

| Purpose | Road Signs | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Add information | **Furthermore, moreover, in addition** | The exchanger is compact. **Furthermore**, it provides a large heat-transfer area. |
| Change direction | **However, nevertheless, although** | The equipment has several advantages. **However**, it also has limitations. |
| Contrast | **In contrast, on the other hand, while, whereas** | One fluid enters from this side. **In contrast**, the second fluid enters from the opposite side. |
| Result | **Therefore, thus, consequently** | Deposits create additional resistance. **Consequently**, heat-transfer performance can decrease. |

Using these expressions helps the audience recognize the relationship between different pieces of information without having to see written headings.

### Practice

Choose **however, furthermore, in contrast,** or **therefore**.

1. A plate heat exchanger is compact. ____________, it provides a large surface area.
2. Heat exchangers can improve energy recovery. ____________, their performance can be affected by fouling.
3. The hot fluid loses thermal energy. ____________, the cold fluid gains thermal energy.
4. Fouling increases resistance to heat transfer. ____________, heat-transfer performance can decrease.

# 3. Restating Important Ideas

## Learning Objective

Reformulate a technical concept so that it is easier for an audience to understand.

During an oral presentation, listeners cannot easily go back and reread a difficult explanation. Therefore, important or complex information should sometimes be **restated in simpler language**.

Useful expressions include:

- **In other words, ...**
- **In short, ...**
- **In simple terms, ...**
- **What I mean by this is ...**
- **Basically, what this means is ...**

### Example

**Technical statement:**

> Fouling creates additional thermal resistance.

**Restatement:**

> **In simple terms**, deposits on the heat-transfer surface can make heat transfer less effective.

Another example:

> The temperature difference provides the driving force for heat transfer. **In other words**, a temperature difference is necessary for thermal energy to move from the hotter fluid toward the colder fluid.

Restatement does not mean simply repeating the same sentence. The purpose is to express the same idea in a clearer or more accessible way.

### Practice

Restate the following idea in simpler English:

> The accumulation of deposits increases thermal resistance.

Start with:

> **In simple terms, this means that...**

# 4. Using Quotations and Examples

## Learning Objective

Support technical explanations with appropriately introduced sources and concrete examples.

## Quotations

When speaking, quotation marks cannot be seen. Therefore, a quotation needs to be **verbally introduced** so the audience knows that the words or ideas come from another person or publication.

Useful expressions include:

> According to **[author/source]**, ...

> To quote **[author]**, “...”

> **[Author]** states that ...

Any quotation included in a technical presentation should come from an **actual source used during the preparation of the presentation**. Quotations should not be invented.

## Examples

Examples help move the audience from an **abstract concept to a concrete case**.

Useful expressions include:

- **For example, ...**
- **For instance, ...**
- **Let's take the example of ...**
- **An interesting example is ...**
- **Let's take the case where ...**

### Example

> Heat exchangers have many applications in chemical processes. **For example**, they can be used to transfer thermal energy between two process streams.

Examples are particularly useful when explaining operating principles, industrial applications, advantages, limitations, or difficult technical concepts.

### Practice

Explain one application of the selected equipment:

> **Let's take the example of** ________________________________.

> In this application, the equipment ____________________________.

# 5. Expressing Opinions

## Learning Objective

Differentiate personal interpretation from another person's opinion.

Not every statement in a presentation needs to begin with expressions such as **“I think”** or **“in my opinion.”** Opinion markers are most useful when it is necessary to distinguish between different viewpoints or to make it clear that a statement represents an interpretation rather than an established fact.

### Another Person's Opinion

> **According to [author], ...**

> **[Author] has expressed the opinion that ...**

### A Commonly Held Opinion

> **It is commonly thought that ...**

### The Speaker's Opinion

> **In my opinion, ...**

> **I think that ...**

> **It is my view that ...**

> **It seems to me that ...**

### Chemical Engineering Example

> **In my opinion**, one of the most important advantages of this heat exchanger is __________ because __________.

### Important Language Rule

Use:

> **According to + another person/source**

Do not use:

> **According to me**

When presenting a personal opinion, expressions such as **“in my opinion”** or **“I think that”** are appropriate.

# 6. Presenting Numbers

## Learning Objective

Present numerical information in a way that listeners can process easily.

Complicated numbers can be difficult to understand when they are heard only once. When an exact number is unnecessary for the point being made, it can be more effective to provide an **approximation** and clearly indicate that the value is approximate.

Useful expressions include:

| Expression | Example |
|---|---|
| **approximately** | approximately 50 °C |
| **nearly** | nearly 100 units |
| **roughly** | roughly 25% |
| **over** | over 200 units |
| **more than** | more than 30% |

For example, instead of saying:

> The heat-transfer area is **47.863 square meters**.

when that degree of precision is unnecessary, the speaker could say:

> The heat-transfer area is **approximately 48 square meters**.

The appropriate level of precision depends on the purpose of the information. Exact values should be retained when precision is important to the technical explanation.

**Oral presentation:**

# 7. Explaining Charts, Graphs, and Tables

## Learning Objective

Introduce, describe, and interpret technical visual information.

A graph, chart, diagram, or table should be **introduced before the audience is expected to examine it**. Once the visual appears, the speaker should explain what it represents instead of leaving the audience to interpret all the information independently.

A useful three-step structure is:

## Step 1 — Introduce

Direct the audience's attention toward the visual.

> **Next, I'd like you to look at this graph showing...**

Other useful expressions include:

> **This graph illustrates...**

> **Let's now look at this diagram...**

> **The table on the screen shows...**

## Step 2 — Describe

Identify the important elements of the visual.

For a graph:

> **The horizontal axis represents...**

> **The vertical axis represents...**

> **The solid line represents...**

For a table:

> **The first column shows...**

> **The values in this row represent...**

For a diagram:

> **This section represents...**

> **The arrows indicate...**

## Step 3 — Interpret

Explain what the information means and why it matters.

Avoid simply saying:

> The line goes down.

Instead, explain the **meaning** of the change:

> As the hot fluid moves through the heat exchanger, its temperature decreases.

The objective is not simply to describe visible upward or downward movement. Important changes should be identified and their technical significance explained.

Common visual formats used in technical presentations include:

- **Bar charts**
- **Pie charts**
- **Tables**
- **Line graphs**
- **Flow charts**
- **Technical diagrams**

### Practice

Select one graph, diagram, or table related to the assigned equipment and prepare three statements.

> **Introduction:** I'd like you to look at ________________________________.

> **Description:** This graph/table/diagram shows __________________________.

> **Interpretation:** The most important point here is ______________________.

# Final Speaking Task

Prepare the **body of an individual oral presentation** about one piece of heat-transfer equipment.

A possible organization is:

1. **Main components**
2. **Operating principle**
3. **Heat-transfer process**
4. **Important operating variables**
5. **Industrial applications**
6. **Advantages and limitations**
7. **Relevant graph, diagram, or table**

During the presentation, demonstrate the following seven communication skills:

1. **Transitions**
2. **Road signs**
3. **Restatement**
4. **Quotations and examples**
5. **Opinions**
6. **Numbers**
7. **Explanation of visuals**

The presentation should not sound like a collection of isolated technical facts. Each section should connect logically to the next, and the audience should be able to recognize where the presentation is going.
